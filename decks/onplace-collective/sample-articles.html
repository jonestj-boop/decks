Community amenities that sell homes: what developers are learning

Every master plan has a pool, a clubhouse and a trail. The communities that sell fastest treat amenities as the start of a way of life, not a line on the site plan.

Ask a buyer why they chose one master-planned community over another and they rarely say "the pool was bigger." They talk about the farmers market where they met their neighbors, the pickleball group that adopted them in week one, or the trail their kids ride to school. The amenity mattered, but what sold them was what happened there.

That gap between building an amenity and building a reason to use it is where many developers lose money. Over three seasons of Smith Lane, homebuilders, operators and designers have described how they close it. Here's what they've learned.

Start with who's buying, not what competitors built

The most common amenity mistake is copying the community down the road. Kelly Mangum, who leads design for model homes, clubhouses and welcome centers at Builders Design, builds every project around a buyer profile. A clubhouse for young families and a clubhouse for active adults may share a footprint, but they shouldn't share a program.

OnPlace founder Monaca Smith Onstad makes the same point from the planning side: the placemaking process starts by learning what a community wants, then building around that vision. An amenity list written before that research is a guess.

Developer question to ask early: who are the first 200 buyers, and what will they do on a Tuesday night? Plan the amenity around that answer.

Build amenities around a way of life

Some of the strongest recent master plans are organized around one idea instead of a checklist. Pulte's Central Florida team described Grow, a 2,000-home agrihood centered on a working farm and preserved oaks. Mattamy Homes built Newfield around preserved land, a working farm and a town center. In both cases the anchor amenity tells buyers what kind of life the community is for.

An anchor like that does marketing work too. It gives sales teams a story, gives residents something to be proud of, and gives programming a natural home.

Design amenities for gathering

An amenity can be busy and still not build community. People swim laps and leave. The amenities that create connection give people a reason to linger and a way to meet. Pulte's Everbee community pairs a "party pickleball pavilion" with barefoot bars and walkable event lawns, so a game turns into a hangout.

Ryan Rosen of Friendly Futures pushed back on what he called the myth of "just add amenities." His point: programming matters as much as the physical space, and small touches of surprise and delight do more for everyday interaction than another square foot of clubhouse.

In practice, that means sightlines from the pool deck to the pavilion, seating where people naturally pause, shade where parents wait, and room for a food truck on a Friday night. None of these show up on a typical amenity checklist, and all of them decide whether people stay.

Staff the gym and program it

Fitness centers are one of the most expected amenities and one of the most underused. Graham Anderson, co-owner of Anchor Fitness and Performance, argues the difference is people. His team listens to resident feedback, hires instructors who care about members, and chooses equipment and classes for each community's mix of ages and abilities. A staffed fitness program becomes a social hub. An empty room of machines becomes a line item.

Rosen made a related point about wellness: the fanciest gym doesn't make residents well. The quality of their relationships and their sense of belonging do. A group run club that meets at the trailhead may do more for resident health than a new piece of equipment.

Design clubhouses for year five, not only for the tour

Model homes and clubhouses carry a lot of weight in the sales process, so it's tempting to design them for the first impression. Kelly Mangum's approach at Builders Design is to make spaces that impress on a walk-through and still meet residents' needs for years. That means durable finishes, flexible rooms that can host a book club one night and a board meeting the next, and furniture chosen for how people will use the room.

Expectations keep shifting, especially in multifamily and build-to-rent communities, where residents compare amenities across properties before they sign a lease. A clubhouse that works on day one and adapts by year five protects the developer's reputation long after the sales team moves on.

Make premium amenities open to everyone

Golf used to be the classic exclusive amenity. Brad Compton, president of ICON Management Services, described the bundled golf model, where every resident has membership benefits. It makes golf more accessible and more affordable than a traditional equity club, and it turns the course into a community space. ICON-managed communities see close to 50,000 rounds a year, with clinics and events built for women golfers and juniors.

The lesson applies beyond golf. When an amenity is part of every home's value, residents use it, and use builds community.

Test with small moves before big builds

Not every idea deserves a building. Monaca and Callie Friedman spent an episode on farmers markets, which can show buyers what living in a community feels like without a sales pitch. They also flagged the risks: in smaller communities, a lack of vendors or attendance can sink a market. Research the demand and find a skilled operator before committing.

Newfield shows the upside of getting it right. Mattamy launched its farmers market and community events early, to win over the surrounding area long before the first home was built.

Decide who runs each amenity before it opens

An amenity without an operator is a maintenance bill. Before construction, decide who will staff it, who will program it, and how that's funded once the developer hands the community to the HOA. The bundled golf model works because the operating plan is part of the design. The same thinking applies to a pool, a farm or a fitness center.

It also helps to agree on how you'll measure success. Simple signals work: how many residents use each space in a typical week, how many attend events, what new residents say during move-in, and which amenities buyers mention on tours. Those numbers tell you where to add programming and what to change in the next phase.

An amenity planning checklist

  1. Define the first buyers and how they spend their week.
  2. Choose one anchor amenity that tells the community's story.
  3. Design every space for lingering and meeting, not only for use.
  4. Budget for staffing and programming, not only construction.
  5. Open premium amenities to all residents where the model allows.
  6. Test new ideas with events and pop-ups before you build.
  7. Plan who operates each amenity before it opens.

Frequently asked questions

What amenities do buyers want in a master-planned community?

It depends on who's buying. Families, active adults and renters want different things. The communities that perform best match amenities to a defined buyer profile and plan the programming that keeps them in use.

Do more amenities sell more homes?

Not on their own. Smith Lane guests repeatedly point to programming, staffing and design for gathering as what turns an amenity into a reason to buy.

When should amenity planning start?

Before the land plan is final. Early decisions about where amenities sit and who runs them shape how residents use them for decades.

8 placemaking examples developers can learn from

Placemaking is easy to define and hard to do. These real projects, shared by guests on Smith Lane, show what it looks like when a place gives people a reason to come back.

Placemaking is the work of turning a space into a place people care about. For developers, it's also a business decision. Places with a strong identity sell faster, hold value and build the kind of word of mouth no ad budget can buy.

The examples below come from conversations on Smith Lane, OnPlace's podcast about the people behind lasting communities. Each one shows a different way in.

What placemaking means for a developer

The word gets used for everything from public art to street furniture. For a master-planned community, it covers three layers that work together:

  • Identity. A clear story about what this place is and who it's for, often drawn from the land or the region.
  • Physical design. Spaces laid out so people cross paths, pause and gather.
  • Programming. The events, routines and people that give residents a reason to use those spaces together.

Most projects invest heavily in the second layer and lightly in the other two. The examples below show what happens when all three line up.

1. Indigolf at Indigo: a game woven through the community

Ryan Rosen's studio, Friendly Futures, designed Indigolf, a golf-inspired game spread throughout the Indigo master-planned community. Instead of one amenity in one spot, the game sends residents across the community and into conversation with each other.

What to borrow: play works for every age. Rosen points to research on play as a lifelong wellness tool, and treats it as a design input, not an afterthought.

2. Cowboy Golf at Fox Point Farms

Friendly Futures built a different game, Cowboy Golf, for Fox Point Farms in San Diego. Same idea, local character. The game reflects the place, which is why residents adopt it.

What to borrow: a custom experience beats an off-the-shelf one. The same studio has tailored games for Chicago's Wild Mile and DFW's Painted Tree.

3. Two Step Farm: a community built on local history

Tom Woliver of Oxland Group described Two Step Farm, a planned 4,000-home community in the Houston market inspired by early Texas settlements that centered on farming, music and dance halls. Oxland's earlier Painted Tree community in McKinney sold 448 homes in its 2023 grand opening year and landed on both the John Burns Top 50 Master Plans and RCLCO's top-selling master-planned communities lists.

A theme like that gives every later decision a filter. Street names, event calendars, the look of the welcome center and even the music at the grand opening can all tie back to the same story, so the community feels like one place instead of a collection of amenities.

What to borrow: the most authentic theme is often already in the land's history. Find it before you invent one.

4. Newfield: activation before the first home

Mattamy Homes started building community at Newfield long before it built houses. Division President Dan Grosswald explained how a farmers market and community events earned buy-in from surrounding residents early, around a plan anchored by preserved land, a working farm and a town center.

What to borrow: early activation does two jobs. It wins over neighbors during entitlement and gives future buyers something real to visit.

5. Columbia Parc: changing a neighborhood's story

Stephanie Darden Bennett of Prismatic, who calls her work "place whispering," helped redefine Columbia Parc in New Orleans, a post-Katrina neighborhood with a hard reputation, as a thriving mixed-income community. It still has a waitlist. She also makes the case for temporary uses like pop-ups and experiential marketing on dormant land, so community forms before a site is fully built.

What to borrow: brand and experience are part of placemaking. How people talk about a place shapes who shows up.

6. Bike Bus: a routine that becomes a ritual

Jessica Tillyer co-founded Bike Bus World after looking for a community-driven way to bike her kids to school. A group ride to school turned into a movement, and a weekday routine became a reason for neighbors to know each other.

A routine like this works because it repeats. The same families meet at the same corner on the same mornings, and over a school year those short rides add up to real relationships between kids, parents and neighbors who might never have met otherwise.

What to borrow: some of the best placemaking costs almost nothing. Design trails and streets so routines like this can happen, then help residents start them. A lifestyle director who recruits the first five families can turn a good idea into a habit.

7. Small, playful touches in public space

Paul Kalbfleisch, co-author of The Joy Experiments, shared how small touches, like a few spinning chairs at about $2,000, can spark more belonging than a million-dollar installation. He also talked about murals and collective storytelling as ways to give residents ownership of a place.

What to borrow: spend some of the budget on invitations to play and participate, not only on hardscape.

8. Casa Airbnb: designing for micro-interactions

Interior architect Rebecca Fairman helped create Casa Airbnb at the Olympics, where design and neuroscience were used to encourage interaction. Approachable activities like pin-making and communal fire pits gave strangers an easy reason to talk.

What to borrow: make the first interaction small and low pressure. A fire pit does more than a mixer.

What these examples have in common

  • They start with people and local character instead of a standard amenity list.
  • They give residents something to do together, which matters more than somewhere to be.
  • They begin early, often before the first home closes.
  • Many of the strongest moves are inexpensive.

How to choose a placemaking approach for your community

No single example above fits every project. A few questions help narrow it down:

  1. What's already here? History, landscape, local culture and nearby neighbors all suggest a direction. Two Step Farm drew on early Texas settlements. Newfield built around preserved land and a working farm.
  2. Who's coming first? Young families respond to routines like a bike bus or a weekend market. Active adults may want clubs, classes and social golf.
  3. What can start before construction ends? Pop-ups, markets and events on dormant land build a following while the site is still dirt.
  4. Who will keep it going? A game, a market or a mural program needs an owner. Budget for the people who run it along with the installation.

The goal is a place residents describe in their own words. When buyers start telling a community's story for you, placemaking is working.

Frequently asked questions

What is placemaking in real estate development?

It's the planning, design and programming that give a community a distinct identity and a reason for people to gather. It covers physical spaces and the experiences that happen in them.

How early should placemaking start?

As early as possible. Newfield's farmers market and Columbia Parc's pop-up approach both show the value of building community before construction is finished.

Does placemaking have to be expensive?

No. Several of the examples above, from Bike Bus to a few playful chairs, cost very little compared to a new amenity building.

What is social architecture? Designing communities around how people feel

Developers plan lot sizes, roads and amenities in detail. Social architecture applies the same rigor to the part buyers remember most: how it feels to live there.

A working definition

Social architecture is the intentional design of the systems, interactions and environments that create meaningful human connection. In a master-planned community, that means planning amenities, programming, staffing and even the sales experience around one question: will people here know and rely on each other?

OnPlace Collective calls this Social Architecture™ and builds its work around it. As Monaca Smith Onstad and Callie Friedman put it on Smith Lane, the feeling is the feature.

The idea goes beyond traditional lifestyle programming. A lifestyle calendar is one output. Social architecture also covers where amenities sit on the land plan, how the welcome center greets a first-time visitor, how a new resident gets introduced to neighbors, and who is responsible for keeping that going after the last home sells. Monaca and Callie drew examples from places as different as retail fitting rooms, fitness centers and farmers markets to show how small design and service choices change the way people feel in a space.

Why it's moving from nice-to-have to required

Amenity packages have converged. Most new communities offer some version of the same pool, clubhouse and trail system. What buyers can't compare on a brochure is whether they'll belong. Monaca and Callie argue that social architecture now affects both sales and resident satisfaction, which makes it a development decision, not a marketing afterthought.

Charles Vogl, author of The Art of Community, sharpened the point on Smith Lane. He separates "mirage" communities, which amount to a list of residents and events, from communities with deep bonds that hold up when life gets hard. Lists, events and amenities alone don't create the second kind.

The science behind the feeling

Social architecture overlaps with neuroarchitecture, the study of how built environments affect the brain and behavior. Rebecca Fairman, an interior architect whose master's thesis focused on neuroarchitecture, shared an "Architecture of Connection" framework built on three ideas:

  • Multisensory design. Spaces should engage more than the eyes. Fairman describes it as designing for the whole body.
  • Approachable micro-interactions. Small, low-pressure reasons to talk, like a communal fire pit or a simple hands-on activity.
  • Local stories. Spaces that reflect the place and its people feel welcoming instead of generic.

Her work also extends to trauma-informed design for transitional housing, a reminder that how a space feels has real effects on the people in it.

What it looks like on the ground

Social architecture shows up in decisions large and small:

  • Invitations over announcements. Vogl argues that personal, inefficient invitations work far better than mass emails. Programming plans should budget for people who know residents by name.
  • Room for quiet connection. Vogl also recommends small, screen-free spaces where trust can grow, not only large event venues.
  • Play and joy as design inputs. Paul Kalbfleisch makes the case that joy is the missing ingredient in resilient places, and that playful touches can matter more than expensive installations.
  • Amenities activated around people. PulteGroup's Central Florida team described activating amenities around real residents rather than checklists.

Social architecture sells homes

Buyers can tour a model home in an afternoon. What they can't see on a tour is whether anyone will wave when they walk the dog. Communities that plan for connection give the sales team something real to show: a full calendar, a market on the lawn, residents who stop to talk with prospects. A Communities Connected podcast episode featuring Monaca and Callie put it plainly: your lifestyle director might be the best salesperson on your site.

That's why OnPlace treats the sales experience as part of social architecture. The welcome center, the first event a buyer attends and the way new residents are introduced to neighbors all shape whether people choose the community and whether they stay.

Common mistakes

  • Treating lifestyle as a marketing line. A brochure promise of "community" with no staff or budget behind it erodes trust fast.
  • Planning programming after the amenities are built. By then the spaces may not support the activities residents want.
  • Only big events. A huge holiday festival is memorable, but weekly routines do more to help neighbors know each other.
  • Copying another community's calendar. Programming should reflect who lives there and the place's own story.
  • No owner after handoff. If no one is responsible for connection once the developer leaves, it fades.

Questions to ask at the earliest stage of a project

  1. Who will live here first, and what do they need from their neighbors?
  2. Where will residents run into each other without planning to?
  3. What's the first small, low-pressure way a new resident can meet someone?
  4. Who is responsible for programming, and is it in the operating budget?
  5. How will the sales experience show buyers what belonging here feels like?
  6. What local story should the place tell?

Who owns social architecture on a project team

On most projects, connection falls between roles. The land planner places the amenity center, the architect designs it, the marketing team names it, and the HOA inherits it. Nobody owns the question of whether residents will know each other.

Social architecture works best when someone holds that question from the first visioning session through handoff. That person, or partner, reviews the land plan for natural gathering points, shapes the amenity program, aligns the sales experience with the community's story, and builds the programming and staffing plan the HOA will carry forward. It's the reason Monaca and Callie argue developers should be asking about connection at the earliest stages of a project, when changes cost the least.

How to tell if it's working

Connection is harder to count than lots sold, but it leaves signals. Track how many residents attend more than one event, how many resident-led clubs form, how new residents describe the community after their first few months, and how often buyers say they heard about the community from someone who lives there. Over time, those signals show up in referrals, resident satisfaction and resale demand.

How it connects to amenities, marketing and programming

Social architecture runs through every stage of a community. It shapes how amenities are planned (see community amenities that sell homes), how the community is marketed and sold, and how it's activated after move-in (see placemaking examples). OnPlace organizes its three companies around those stages: OnMenity for amenity planning, OnSpire for marketing and sales, and OnVie for programming.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between social architecture and placemaking?

Placemaking focuses on giving a place identity and reasons to gather. Social architecture is the broader design of the systems and interactions that create connection, including staffing, programming and the sales experience.

What is neuroarchitecture?

It's the study of how the built environment affects the brain and behavior. Designers use it to create spaces that feel welcoming and encourage interaction.

Is social architecture only for large master-planned communities?

No. The same principles apply to build-to-rent communities, mixed-use districts and smaller neighborhoods.
