Community amenities that sell homes: what developers are learning

Every master plan has a pool, a clubhouse and a trail. The communities that sell fastest treat amenities as the start of a way of life, not a line on the site plan.

Ask a buyer why they chose one master-planned community over another and they rarely say "the pool was bigger." They talk about the farmers market where they met their neighbors, the pickleball group that adopted them in week one, or the trail their kids ride to school. The amenity mattered, but what sold them was what happened there.

That gap between building an amenity and building a reason to use it is where many developers lose money. Over three seasons of Smith Lane, homebuilders, operators and designers have described how they close it. Here's what they've learned.

Start with who's buying, not what competitors built

OnPlace founder Monaca Smith Onstad spent 20 years on the development side before starting the company. She started it because she watched amenities get designed backwards. Developers built the clubhouse, handed it to a lifestyle director, and said, in effect, go plan some events. The space often didn't fit what residents wanted to do.

Pulte's Central Florida sales leaders described the same shift inside their company. They used to get amenity drawings back with an architect's guess about how buyers would use each space. Now the sales team and lifestyle directors weigh in early, and features that sat unused in past amenities get cut. As Blake Lapinsky, a Pulte vice president of sales, put it: "Let's talk about the lifestyle and activities that we want and build the amenity around that."

Kelly Mangum, who leads design for model homes, clubhouses and welcome centers at Builders Design, works the same way. A clubhouse for young families and a clubhouse for active adults may share a footprint, but they shouldn't share a program.

Developer question to ask early: who are the first 200 buyers, and what will they do on a Tuesday night? Plan the amenity around that answer.

Build amenities around a way of life

Some of the strongest recent master plans are organized around one idea instead of a checklist. Pulte's Grow, east of Orlando, is a 2,000-home agrihood built around a 9-acre working farm, with about 12 miles of trails through preserved wetlands and old oaks. It took 12 years to bring to market. Pulte VP Matt True said a small first section of the farm had already produced about 3,000 pounds of food within months of opening.

Keeping the trees paid off right away. On one visit, True saw three groups of real estate agents filming videos, and none of them were at the model homes. They were all in front of a trail, a tree save or the farm. Even an old pickup the land team painted blue has become the community's favorite photo spot.

Mattamy Homes built Newfield, on Florida's east coast, around preserved land, a working farm and a town center. In both cases the anchor tells buyers what kind of life the community is for and gives the sales team a story to tell.

Design amenities for gathering

An amenity can be busy and still not build community. People swim laps and leave. The amenities that create connection give people a reason to stay and a way to meet. Pulte's plan for the main amenity at EverBe, a 3,300-plus home community in East Orlando, adds a "party pickleball pavilion" next to the courts, because half the fun of pickleball is talking while you wait for a court. A barefoot bar sits off the pool, with a playground and splash pad close by, so parents can sit down and still see every kid.

Ryan Rosen of Friendly Futures, a studio that designs places to play, says well-designed parks and landscapes often fall short because most people won't start a conversation with a stranger. Some amenities help more than others. Pickleball puts four people close together at the kitchen line, so they talk. Pools and trails get plenty of use, but people don't talk much there. Rosen's advice is to pair structured play, with clear rules, and open-ended play in the same places.

In practice, that means sightlines from the pool deck to the pavilion, seating where people naturally pause, shade where parents wait, and room for a food truck on a Friday night. None of these show up on a typical amenity checklist, and all of them decide whether people stay.

Staff the gym and program it

Buyers ask about fitness more than almost anything else. OnPlace's Callie Friedman said "is there a fitness center?" comes up on tour after tour, and Pulte's team sees wellness, from gyms to walking trails and parks, as the most common request across its communities.

A gym full of machines still isn't a reason to buy. Graham Anderson, co-owner of Anchor Fitness and Performance, argues the difference is people. His team listens to resident feedback, hires instructors who care about members, and picks equipment and classes for each community's mix of ages and abilities. A staffed fitness program becomes a social hub. An empty room becomes a line item.

Rosen points to the Harvard Study of Adult Development, which found that relationships are the biggest factor in living a good life. A run club that meets at the trailhead may do more for resident health than a new piece of equipment.

Design clubhouses for year five, not only for the tour

Model homes and clubhouses carry a lot of weight in the sales process, so it's tempting to design them for the first impression. Kelly Mangum's approach is to make spaces that impress on a walk-through and still meet residents' needs for years. That means durable finishes, flexible rooms that can host a book club one night and a board meeting the next, and furniture chosen for how people will use the room.

Make premium amenities open to everyone

Golf used to be the classic exclusive amenity. Brad Compton, president of ICON Management Services, described the bundled golf model, where every resident has membership benefits. It makes golf cheaper and easier to join than a traditional equity club, and it turns the course into a community space. ICON-managed communities see close to 50,000 rounds a year, with clinics and events built for women golfers and juniors.

Pulte is trying a version of this at Lake Haven, in the Winter Garden area west of Orlando. About 500 Del Webb active-adult homes sit on one side of the lake with their own private amenity center, and Pulte homes sit on the other, about 1,400 homes in all. Del Webb residents can also use the main amenity, which has indoor pickleball, golf simulators and a movie lawn on the water. The team said it's the first time they've shared a main amenity that way. The grand opening drew more than 200 people, with more than 50 homes already sold.

The lesson applies beyond golf. When an amenity is part of every home's value, residents use it, and use builds community.

Test with small moves before big builds

Not every idea deserves a building. At EverBe, before the first real amenity opened, Pulte left a small event lawn between the sales center and the model homes. It was meant as a showpiece. The lifestyle director turned it into the community's gathering spot, and Lapinsky said it drew more activity and resident buy-in than $10 million amenities in other communities.

Farmers markets are another low-cost test. Monaca and Callie spent an episode on them: a market can show buyers what living in a community feels like without a sales pitch, but in smaller communities a lack of vendors or attendance can sink it. Research the demand and find a skilled operator first. At Newfield, OnPlace helped launch a market in the middle of the site while it was still bare dirt. It's still running, and residents now tend their own raised beds through a farm club.

Decide who runs each amenity before it opens

A Del Webb lifestyle leader gave the Pulte team a line that stuck: the amenities are the canvas, and the people and activity are the painting. An amenity without an operator is a maintenance bill. Before construction, decide who will staff it, who will program it, and how that's funded once the developer hands the community to the HOA. The bundled golf model works because the operating plan is part of the design.

It also helps to agree on how you'll measure success. Simple signals work: how many residents use each space in a typical week, how many attend events, what new residents say during move-in, and which amenities buyers mention on tours.

An amenity planning checklist

  1. Define the first buyers and how they spend their week.
  2. Bring sales and lifestyle staff into amenity design early.
  3. Choose one anchor amenity that tells the community's story.
  4. Design every space for lingering and meeting, not only for use.
  5. Budget for staffing and programming, not only construction.
  6. Test new ideas with events and pop-ups before you build.
  7. Plan who operates each amenity before it opens.

Frequently asked questions

What amenities do buyers want in a master-planned community?

It depends on who's buying, but wellness comes up everywhere: fitness centers, trails, parks and places to get outside. The communities that perform best match amenities to a defined buyer profile and plan the programming that keeps them in use.

Do more amenities sell more homes?

Not on their own. At EverBe, a small event lawn with a good lifestyle director drew more activity than $10 million amenities elsewhere. Programming, staffing and design for gathering turn an amenity into a reason to buy.

When should amenity planning start?

Before the land plan is final. Early decisions about where amenities sit and who runs them shape how residents use them for decades.

8 placemaking examples developers can learn from

Placemaking is easy to define and hard to do. These real projects, shared by guests on Smith Lane, show what it looks like when a place gives people a reason to come back.

Placemaking is the work of turning a space into a place people care about. For developers, it's also a business decision. Places with a strong identity sell faster, hold value and build the kind of word of mouth no ad budget can buy.

The examples below come from conversations on Smith Lane, OnPlace's podcast about the people behind lasting communities. Each one shows a different way in.

What placemaking means for a developer

The word gets used for everything from public art to street furniture. For a master-planned community, it covers three layers that work together:

  • Identity. A clear story about what this place is and who it's for, often drawn from the land or the region.
  • Physical design. Spaces laid out so people cross paths, pause and gather.
  • Programming. The events, routines and people that give residents a reason to use those spaces together.

Most projects invest heavily in the second layer and lightly in the other two. The examples below show what happens when all three line up.

1. Indigolf at Indigo: a game woven through the community

Ryan Rosen's studio, Friendly Futures, designs what he calls placemaking games: new games built to get neighbors talking. Indigolf, at the Indigo master-planned community, has 14 holes, a front seven and a back seven, spread across the neighborhood. Only one hole uses a real putter, a course through a fitness park. At another, you roll a bocce ball through a reading area with a giant hammock. The zen garden becomes a memory game of matching rocks. On the lake trail, spotting an eagle earns you an eagle. If someone nearby takes a shot for you, your score improves, so players have an excuse to talk to whoever is around. The scorecard doubles as a postcard you can drop in a neighbor's mailbox.

What to borrow: Rosen's rule is that "friendships are a byproduct of proximity and repetition." A neighborhood supplies the proximity. A game people play again and again supplies the repetition, and it gets more use out of amenities the developer already paid for.

2. Cowboy Golf at Fox Point Farms

At Fox Point Farms, an agrihood in San Diego, Friendly Futures wove Cowboy Golf through the farm, brewery and restaurant. Players swing a broom topped with a toy horse head, and a nearby "saloon social" area adds cowboy-themed lawn games.

What to borrow: a game that fits the place gets adopted. The same studio built a seed-ball target game along the trail at Chicago's Wild Mile riverfront park, designed to pull visitors past the first stretch and down the whole trail.

3. Two Step Farm and Painted Tree: building on local history

Tom Woliver of Oxland Group described Two Step Farm, a planned 4,000-home community in the Houston market inspired by early Texas settlements that centered on farming, music and dance halls. Oxland's earlier Painted Tree community in McKinney sold 448 homes in its 2023 grand opening year and landed on both the John Burns Top 50 Master Plans and RCLCO's top-selling master-planned communities lists.

Painted Tree also shows how far a small budget can go. Instead of a multimillion-dollar sports complex on a flood plain, Friendly Futures built a field of games. It includes a hybrid cricket and baseball field for a community with many South Asian families and native Texans, with home run lines set by age and ability. The outer boundary doubles as an eighth-mile walking loop, and stones in the painted rock garden double as pieces for backgammon, mancala and other board games.

What to borrow: the most authentic theme is often already in the land's history or in the people moving in. Find it before you invent one.

4. Newfield: activation before the first home

Mattamy Homes started building community at Newfield, on Florida's east coast, long before it built houses. Division President Dan Grosswald explained how a farmers market and community events earned buy-in from surrounding residents early. OnPlace helped launch that market in the middle of the site while it was still bare dirt. It's still running, and residents now tend their own raised beds through a farm club.

What to borrow: early activation does two jobs. It wins over neighbors during entitlement and gives future buyers something real to visit.

5. Columbia Parc: changing a neighborhood's story

Stephanie Darden Bennett of Prismatic, who calls her work "place whispering," helped redefine Columbia Parc in New Orleans, a post-Katrina neighborhood with a hard reputation, as a thriving mixed-income community. It still has a waitlist. She also makes the case for temporary uses like pop-ups and experiential marketing on dormant land, so community forms before a site is fully built.

What to borrow: brand and experience are part of placemaking. How people talk about a place shapes who shows up.

6. Bike Bus: a routine that becomes a ritual

Jessica Tillyer co-founded Bike Bus World after looking for a community-driven way to bike her kids to school. A group ride to school turned into a movement, and a weekday routine became a reason for neighbors to know each other. The same families meet at the same corner on the same mornings, and over a school year those short rides add up to real relationships.

What to borrow: some of the best placemaking costs almost nothing. Design trails and streets so routines like this can happen, then help residents start them.

7. Sponge chairs at the Gaslight District

Paul Kalbfleisch co-wrote The Joy Experiments with developer Scott Higgins, drawing on their work at the Gaslight District in Ontario's Waterloo Region. The district opened with three large installations that cost close to $1 million or more each. Kalbfleisch also spent about $10,000 on ten spinning "sponge chairs" for the courtyard. Today people call it "the place with the chairs." Each chair rocks back far enough that you're sure you'll tip over, so people scream, laugh at themselves and then laugh with the strangers next to them. He calls it small play, and compares it to small talk.

What to borrow: Kalbfleisch's own rule, "design the invitation, not the rules." He points to St. Pete Pier in Florida, where steps down to the beach were built as bleachers so people can sit and watch others play.

8. Casa Airbnb: designing for micro-interactions

At the Milan Winter Olympics, interior architect Rebecca Fairman led design for Casa Airbnb, a hospitality house meant to get strangers talking. Visitors could make their own trading pins at a communal craft table, sitting side by side instead of face to face, which she says takes the pressure off a first conversation. A garden fountain became a fire pit ringed with chairs, blankets and heat lamps. Local makers furnished the house, a local DJ collective played, and local coffee experts poured the espresso. In a post-event survey, visitors called it their favorite part of the Olympics.

What to borrow: make the first interaction small and low pressure. OnPlace hears pushback on fire pits in Florida. Keep them anyway: a fire is where people sit down and tell stories.

What these examples have in common

  • They start with people and local character instead of a standard amenity list.
  • They give residents something to do together, which matters more than somewhere to be.
  • They begin early, often before the first home closes.
  • Many of the strongest moves are inexpensive.

How to choose a placemaking approach for your community

  1. What's already here? History, landscape, local culture and nearby neighbors all suggest a direction.
  2. Who's coming first? Young families respond to routines like a bike bus or a weekend market. Active adults may want clubs, classes and social golf.
  3. What can start before construction ends? Pop-ups, markets and events on dormant land build a following while the site is still dirt.
  4. Who will keep it going? A game, a market or a mural program needs an owner. Budget for the people who run it along with the installation.

The goal is a place residents describe in their own words. When buyers start telling a community's story for you, placemaking is working.

Frequently asked questions

What is placemaking in real estate development?

It's the planning, design and programming that give a community a distinct identity and a reason for people to gather. It covers physical spaces and the experiences that happen in them.

How early should placemaking start?

As early as possible. Newfield's farmers market started on bare dirt, before any homes were built.

Does placemaking have to be expensive?

No. Ten sponge chairs at the Gaslight District cost about $10,000 and became what visitors remember most.

Social Architecture · 9 min read

What is social architecture? Designing communities around how people feel

Developers plan lot sizes, roads and amenities in detail. Social architecture applies the same rigor to the part buyers remember most: how it feels to live there.

A working definition

Social architecture is the intentional design of the systems, interactions and environments that create meaningful human connection. In a master-planned community, that means planning amenities, programming, staffing and even the sales experience around one question: will people here know and rely on each other?

OnPlace Collective calls this Social Architecture™ and builds its work around it. As Monaca Smith Onstad and Callie Friedman put it on Smith Lane, the feeling is the feature.

Charles Vogl, author of The Art of Community, gave Smith Lane a simple test for whether it's working. He defines a community as "a group of people who share mutual concern for one another." Living on the same street doesn't qualify on its own, and neither does liking the same music. What counts is whether people care what happens to each other.

Why it's moving from nice-to-have to required

Amenity packages have converged. Most new communities offer some version of the same pool, clubhouse and trail system. What buyers can't compare on a brochure is whether they'll belong.

The industry is noticing. At recent John Burns and Zonda conferences, Callie said nearly every speaker came back to community and connection. Building nice homes with a pool and a clubhouse isn't enough anymore. Loneliness also isn't only a young person's problem. One panelist pointed to men over 60, often after a divorce, as the group struggling most, which is why OnPlace plans programming and spaces for 55-plus communities with them in mind.

Vogl adds a warning. Many groups that call themselves communities are what he calls mirage communities: lists. A list of people who live in a neighborhood is a neighbor list. A list of people who came to an event is an audience list. Neither is a community until people know and look out for each other.

The science behind the feeling

Social architecture overlaps with neuroarchitecture. Rebecca Fairman, an interior architect who leads experiential design at Airbnb and is finishing a master's in neuroscience applied to architecture, defines it as "the study of how the environment around us shapes how we feel, what we think, and what we do." Her Architecture of Connection framework has five parts:

  • Ambient belonging. People should feel welcome the moment they walk in, through local makers, local photos and local recommendations.
  • Design for the whole body. Sound, smell and touch matter as much as what people see.
  • Micro-interaction infrastructure. Low-stakes reasons to talk, like a shared craft table or a fire pit.
  • Layer time into space. Vintage pieces, old books and materials with patina feel safer than a room where everything is brand new.
  • Reflect the people who are there. Bring in locals to run, staff and shape the space.

The effects are measurable. Through the nonprofit Flip4Good, Fairman's team tracked two families in similar units at the same transitional housing facility for a year, using wearables and surveys. The family in the redesigned unit reported close to twice the gains in feeling safe, happy and optimistic.

What it looks like on the ground

  • Invitations over announcements. Vogl says "an invitation is when the person invited knows that someone cares if they don't show up." Anything else is an announcement. The more personal the invite, the more it means, which is why programming plans should budget for people who know residents by name.
  • Room for quiet connection. Vogl's test for a space: could someone lean over and share bad news? If music or screens make that impossible, the room isn't built for trust. He keeps music at about 20% of what people expect.
  • Play as a design input. Paul Kalbfleisch, co-author of The Joy Experiments, ties play to belonging: when you take part in something, you feel valued, and feeling valued is what belonging means. He cited US Chamber of Connection figures showing communities that play together report 28% more social support and 33% higher life satisfaction.
  • Amenities activated around people. At Pulte's EverBe community, a small event lawn run by a strong lifestyle director drew more activity than $10 million amenities elsewhere.

Social architecture sells homes

Buyers can tour a model home in an afternoon. What they can't see on a tour is whether anyone will wave when they walk the dog. Communities that plan for connection give the sales team something real to show: a full calendar, a market on the lawn, residents who stop to talk with prospects. A Communities Connected podcast episode featuring Monaca and Callie put it plainly: your lifestyle director might be the best salesperson on your site.

That's why OnPlace treats the sales experience as part of social architecture. The welcome center, the first event a buyer attends and the way new residents are introduced to neighbors all shape whether people choose the community and whether they stay.

Common mistakes

  • Treating lifestyle as a marketing line. A brochure promise of "community" with no staff or budget behind it erodes trust fast.
  • Planning programming after the amenities are built. By then the spaces may not support the activities residents want.
  • Only big, loud events. The people we call in a crisis are rarely people we met in a crowded room. Weekly routines and small gatherings do more.
  • Judging events by headcount. New lifestyle directors get discouraged when two people show up. But those two neighbors now have each other's numbers, and that's the point.
  • No owner after handoff. If no one is responsible for connection once the developer leaves, it fades.

Questions to ask at the earliest stage of a project

  1. Who will live here first, and what do they need from their neighbors?
  2. Where will residents run into each other without planning to?
  3. What's the first small, low-pressure way a new resident can meet someone?
  4. Who is responsible for programming, and is it in the operating budget?
  5. How will the sales experience show buyers what belonging here feels like?
  6. What local story should the place tell?

Who owns social architecture on a project team

On most projects, connection falls between roles. The land planner places the amenity center, the architect designs it, the marketing team names it, and the HOA inherits it. Nobody owns the question of whether residents will know each other.

Social architecture works best when someone holds that question from the first visioning session through handoff. That person, or partner, reviews the land plan for natural gathering points, shapes the amenity program, aligns the sales experience with the community's story, and builds the programming and staffing plan the HOA will carry forward.

How to tell if it's working

Connection is harder to count than lots sold, but it leaves signals. Track how many residents attend more than one event, how many resident-led clubs form, how new residents describe the community after their first few months, and how often buyers say they heard about the community from someone who lives there.

How it connects to amenities, marketing and programming

Social architecture runs through every stage of a community. It shapes how amenities are planned (see community amenities that sell homes), how the community is marketed and sold, and how it's activated after move-in (see placemaking examples). OnPlace organizes its three companies around those stages: OnMenity for amenity planning, OnSpire for marketing and sales, and OnVie for programming.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between social architecture and placemaking?

Placemaking focuses on giving a place identity and reasons to gather. Social architecture is the broader design of the systems and interactions that create connection, including staffing, programming and the sales experience.

What is neuroarchitecture?

It's the study of how the built environment shapes how people feel, think and act. Designers use it to create spaces that feel welcoming and encourage interaction.

Is social architecture only for large master-planned communities?

No. The same principles apply to build-to-rent communities, mixed-use districts and smaller neighborhoods.
