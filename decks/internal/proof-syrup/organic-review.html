Why this exists

The client is disputing the SEO & content work

Cloud Earl raised three claims. This report checks each against GA4, states plainly where the data agrees with him, where it doesn't, and where GA4 simply can't answer.

"Basically everything we spent failed epically to produce anything resembling a profit on the SEO work."
"All of the organic traffic converts … about 90% less than other traffic."
"Massive amounts of errors and incorrect links scattered through nearly all of it."

Prepared as an internal basis for deciding how to respond — not written to defend a position. If the numbers favor the client, they say so.

Bottom line

The content program built traffic but not revenue. His core complaint holds.

Since revenue tracking began (Mar 2025), the blog/content pages drew 67,824 organic sessions and produced $864 in tracked revenue — a 0.03% conversion rate. Nearly all organic revenue came from branded/commercial pages (homepage, collections, products) that convert at 3.22% and would largely exist without the content. Assisted (first-touch) attribution doesn't rescue it: organic introduces only ~7% more revenue than it closes.

Holds up

The SEO content did not produce meaningful direct revenue. "Converts ~90% less" is directionally right — the blog converts ~99% below commercial organic.

Needs nuance

The content did rank and drive 68k sessions of real visibility. Branded organic is healthy and on par with paid social/direct.

Can't confirm here

The "errors & broken links" claim isn't a GA4 question. It needs a site crawl / GSC coverage report. Pre-Mar-2025 ROI is unmeasurable.

Method & limitations — read first

What this data can and can't say
  • Revenue tracking started March 2025. Before that, GA4 recorded sessions but $0 revenue / 0 transactions — ecommerce tracking wasn't configured. Any content or spend before Mar 2025 cannot be assessed for ROI from this data (neither to blame nor to credit).
  • Attribution is last-click (session-based) unless noted. The assisted-conversion section below uses first-touch attribution to test for hidden top-of-funnel value.
  • "Blog/content" = organic sessions landing on /blogs/ URLs; "commercial" = all other organic (homepage, collections, products, store locator).
  • Broken links / technical errors are out of scope for GA4. Verifying that claim requires a crawl (Screaming Frog) or Search Console coverage data — flagged as a follow-up.

Finding 4 — The fairest counter-argument, tested

Do assisted conversions rescue the content? No — not materially.

Last-click undercounts top-of-funnel content: a reader could return later and buy through another channel. To test that, we compared first-touch (introducer) against last-touch (closer) attribution for organic.

ChannelFirst-touch revLast-touch revIntroducer premium
Organic Search$108,305$101,180+$7,124 (+7%)
Direct (for contrast)$129,291$92,148+$37,143 (+40%)
Email (a closer)$290,809$322,137−$31,328

Organic introduces only ~7% more revenue than it closes (~101 extra transactions over 18 months). And that entire premium is dominated by branded organic, not the blog. Since the whole channel's introducer premium is just ~$7k, that is the ceiling on any blog assist effect — the 67,824 blog sessions cannot be hiding meaningful assisted revenue, or organic's first-touch total would dwarf its last-touch total. It doesn't.

Caveat: isolating assisted revenue to specific blog pages exactly needs GA4 Explore path analysis or BigQuery event-level data — the Data API can't tie a landing page to a later purchase. The channel-level ceiling above already bounds it, but a BigQuery pull can confirm to the dollar if the client presses.

Verdict & recommended posture

How to respond to Cloud Earl

What his claim gets right

  • The SEO content did not produce meaningful direct revenue: $864 on 67,824 sessions (0.03%).
  • "Organic converts ~90% less" is directionally correct — understated, even. The blog converts ~99% below commercial organic.
  • Assisted/brand-halo value, the usual defense, is small here (~7% introducer premium, mostly branded).

What the record should also show

  • The content did rank and build 68k sessions of visibility — the execution worked; the targeting (informational recipe queries over commercial/buyer queries) is what didn't pay back.
  • The brand's commercial organic is healthy (3.22% CVR, ~$100k) and is the #2 revenue channel — organic as a business asset is not broken.
  • Revenue tracking only began Mar 2025, so any earlier work can't be judged either way from GA4.

What we still owe before a final reply

  • The "errors & broken links" claim is unverified. Run a crawl (Screaming Frog) + pull GSC coverage/index data. That needs the client to grant our service account access to their Search Console, or to share their own export.
  • Optional: BigQuery pull to state blog-assisted revenue to the dollar, if the conversation gets adversarial.

Suggested tone: acknowledge the direct-revenue shortfall honestly, reframe it as a fixable targeting problem (commercial-intent content + on-page conversion paths) rather than a failure of SEO as a channel, and separate the unverified technical-errors claim until we've actually crawled the site.
